Konrad Wolff v. United States

IFP petition · United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, No. 16-10237 · judgment October 31, 2017

Certiorari denied · April 23, 2018

Question presented

(1) In Rita v. United States, 551 U.S. 338 (2007), this Court detailed what explanation is required of district judges at sentencing hearings under 18 U.S.C. § 3553. A district judge’s clear decision to adopt the United States Sentencing Commission’s proposed Guideline range does “not necessarily require lengthy explanation.” Rita, 551 U.S. at 356. But when a party “contests the Guidelines sentence generally under § 3553(a)—that is, argues that the Guidelines reflect an unsound judgment, or, for example, that they do not generally treat certain defendant characteristics in the proper way—or argues for departure,” this Court explained more is required. Id. at 357. A judge presented with “nonfrivolous reasons” for rejecting a Guideline range must “normally go further and explain why he has rejected those arguments.” Id. The district court must set “forth enough to satisfy the appellate court that he has considered the parties’ arguments and has a reasoned basis for exercising his own legal decisionmaking authority.” Id. at 356. Shortly after Rita was decided, this Court held in Kimbrough v. United States, 552 U.S. 85 (2007), that a district court could vary from a Guideline range based on categorical policy disagreements with them, and not just on individualized determinations they yield an excessive sentence in a particular case. The first question presented is whether under Rita, a district court must explain its reasons on the record for rejecting a policy-based challenge to the child pornography Guideline, U.S.S.G. § 2G2.2—a controversial Guideline with the highest rate of variance—brought under Kimbrough? This issue is closely related to the issue presented in Chavez-Meza v. United States, No. 17-5639, in which this Court granted review to decide whether a district court is obligated to explain its decision to deny a discretionary sentence reduction in a 18 U.S.C. § 3582(c)(2) proceeding. (2) Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11(c)(1) prohibits a district judge from participating in plea discussions “out of concern that a defendant might be induced to plead guilty rather than risk displeasing the judge” and to “facilitate objective assessments of the voluntariness of a defendant’s plea.” United States V. Davila, 569 U.S. 597, 606 (2013). The second question presented is: in order to find a violation of this prohibition on judicial participation in plea discussions, must a district court initiate the discussion, as the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit held below? Or, as held by the Second, Fifth and Seventh Circuits, is any judicial involvement in plea negotiations—even when initiated by the parties as opposed to a district court—a violation of Rule 11?
