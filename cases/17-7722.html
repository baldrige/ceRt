Melvin Jackson v. United States

IFP petition · United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, No. 17-30387 · judgment November 9, 2017

Certiorari denied · March 19, 2018

Questions presented

This petition concerns whether a court can rely on its own factual determinations regarding criminal offenses for which a defendant was neither charged nor convicted to calculate a higher advisory sentencing range and impose a longer sentence than would otherwise be reasonable under the Sentencing Guidelines. The Fifth Circuit and other Courts of Appeals have rejected constitutional challenges to sentencing enhancements based on uncharged conduct on the ground that a sentencing court is entitled to find by a preponderance of the evidence all facts relevant to the determination of a sentence, as long as the sentence is below the statutory maximum for the convicted offense. However, this issue has been the subject of intense disagreement within circuits, and this Court has never weighed in to settle the debate. Here, Petitioner Melvin Jackson was convicted of two drug offenses and one offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. While the jury’s verdict should have resulted in a Sentencing Guidelines range of 121-151 months imprisonment based on the offenses of conviction, the district court relied on its own factual determinations regarding uncharged criminal conduct to apply a cross reference provision in the felon-in-possession Guidelines and sentence Mr. Jackson under the Guidelines for attempted first-degree murder. As a result, Mr. Jackson received a 30-year sentence for one of the drug convictions. The questions presented are:

  1. Whether the district court’s cross reference of the Petitioner’s felon-in-possession conviction to the uncharged offense of attempted first-degree murder in calculating the Petitioner’s Sentencing Guidelines range was unconstitutional.

  2. Whether a court’s reliance on its own factual determinations regarding uncharged criminal conduct to enhance a defendant’s sentence violates the defendant’s Fifth and Sixth Amendment rights.
