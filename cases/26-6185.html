Antonia Blackwell v. The Pearl

IFP petition · Court of Appeals of Utah, No. 20250324-CA · judgment May 5, 2026

Pending

Question presented

Petitioner, a Black tenant who paid her rent in full and on time every month, disputed undisclosed fees her landlord added to that rent under a lease addendum that she did not sign and whose signature lines, the landlord’s and hers alike, are blank (R. 15; Trial Tr. 107:7-13). She complained of discrimination to the state civil-rights agency. The landlord then refused her rent and made her an offer, set down in writing that day by her then-counsel, to let her stay through June, dismiss the fees to date, and give her a positive reference “in return for you dismissing your Discrimination Claim”; when she refused, it sued to evict her, abandoned the fees in court, and was never required to prove them. (App. H; App. B 29 n.7, 38; App. A at 2.) The Utah trial court held that her dispute of the fees was not protected activity because “disputing lawfully assessed fees” is not protected (App. B U 38), and it never addressed.her agency complaint or the offer; the Court of Appeals declined to review that holding and affirmed on the ground that her nonpayment of the same fees was a legitimate reason to evict her (App. A at 3 n.3). The decision is an unpublished order that no federal court other than this one can review. The lower courts are divided on what Section 3617 requires, and this Court has not spoken. The Seventh Circuit, sitting en banc, has observed that “[c]ourts are split” on whether a Section 3617 claim depends on a violation of another provision of the Act, and held that it does not. Bloch v. Frischholz, 587 F.3d 771, 781-82 (7th Cir. 2009) (en banc). The Sixth Circuit, agreeing, identified the Fifth Circuit as holding the opposite. Hidden VilL, LLC v. City of Lakewood, 734 F.3d 519, 529 (6th Cir. 2013). On the related question whether a complaint is protected when the grievance behind it fails, the federal courts construe Section 3617 together with Title VIPs anti­ retaliation provision, Bloch, 587 F.3d at 779, and the Tenth Circuit, whose rule governs the federal courthouse in the same city, holds that a plaintiff may maintain a retaliation claim “based on a reasonable good-faith belief that the underlying conduct violated Title VII.” Crumpacker v. Kan. Dep’t of Hum. Res., 338 F.3d 1163,1172 (10th Cir. 2003); see Love v. RE/MAX of Am., Inc., 738 F.2d 383, 385 (10th Cir. 1984) (opposition protected when “based on a mistaken good faith belief that Title VII has been violated”). Federal courts applying Section 3617 itself hold the same: a tenant “does not have to establish that the conditions or actions she protested or opposed actually constituted discrimination under the FHA, but only that she had a ‘good faith, reasonable belief” that they did. Kris v. Dusseault Family Revocable Tr. of 2017, No. 18-cv-566-LM, 2019 DNH164, slip op. at 13 (D.N.H. Sept. 24, 2019) (citing Broome v. Biondi, 17 F. Supp. 2d 211, 219 (S.D.N.Y. 1997)). This Court reserved that question in Clark County School District v. Breeden, 532 U.S. 268, 270 (2001) (per curiam), and has never addressed it under Section 3617. The Utah courts applied neither rule: they denied protection because the fee dispute was later resolved against the tenant, on fees the landlord had abandoned in court and was never required to prove, imposed under an addendum whose signature lines are blank. The question presented is: Whether a tenant who has paid her rent in full and on time every month, who in good faith disputes undisclosed fees her landlord has added to that rent, and who then complains of discrimination to a civil-rights agency, loses the protection of the Fair Housing Act’s anti­ retaliation provision, 42 U.S.C. § 3617, where the landlord has made her an offer, recorded in writing, to let her stay through June, dismiss the fees, and forgo eviction in return for dismissing that complaint, and then sues to evict her for nonpayment of fees it abandons in court and never proves.
