Guy Gamble v. Florida

IFP petition · Supreme Court of Florida, No. SC17-1101 · judgment January 29, 2018

Certiorari denied · October 1, 2018

Questions presented

On remand from this Court’s decision in Hurst v. Florida, (hereinafter “Hurst”) the Florida Supreme Court held, as a state constitutional consequence, that a death verdict could not be rendered without unanimous jury findings of the aggravating circumstances proven beyond a reasonable doubt and that the aggravating circumstances outweighed any mitigating circumstances, thereby warranting death. Before Hurst and its progeny, a panel rendered an advisory recommendation for life or death by a simple majority vote. The trial court gave this recommendation great weight while making its own factual findings. After Ring v. Arizona (precursor of Hurst), the Florida Supreme Court found that Ring was satisfied in any case with prior violent felony convictions as aggravators. Therefore, even though the court made factual findings as to the aggravators during the penalty phase, it could also be said that, at some point in time, at least one aggravator had been found by a unanimous jury. This approach failed to recognize that a finding that all aggravators to be weighed must be found beyond a reasonable doubt. Additionally, it failed to recognize that whether the aggravators outweigh the mitigators is also an element that must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt and found by a unanimous jury before someone can be sentenced to death. In Mosley v. State, the Florida Supreme Court held that it would apply both the federal and state jury-trial rights retroactively to inmates whose death sentences had not become final as of June 24, 2002 (the date of Ring v. Arizona), but that it would deny relief to inmates whose death sentences were final on or before that date. A second reason for retroactivity was also mentioned. Under the doctrine of fundamental fairness recognized in James v. State and Mosley v. State, defendants that had preserved Sixth Amendment claims like those granted relief in Ring, should also be entitled to Hurst relief. Petitioner’s appeal was final after Ring, but he properly preserved Ring-like claims for appeal. Nevertheless, he was denied relief.

  1. Was the Petitioner’s right to Equal Protection under the Law violated when the Florida Supreme Court failed to consider and apply their precedent in James v. State and Mosley v. State to his case, violating the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, as well as the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against capricious capital sentencing?

  2. Should Hurst apply retroactively, pursuant to a Teague analysis, where Petitioner was denied Due Process of Law when the trial court did not find beyond a reasonable doubt every element necessary to impose a death sentence, nor did it rely solely on its own factual findings, but gave great weight to a non-unanimous advisory recommendation, violating the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution?
