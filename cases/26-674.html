Questions presented

This Court has long held that “property rights in the land include the right to use, lease and dispose of it for lawful purposes,” and that “the Constitution protects these essential attributes of property.” Terrace v. Thompson, 263 U.S. 197, 215 (1923). For more than a century, it has reviewed local restrictions on those rights by asking whether, on the record, the restriction bears a rational relationship to a legitimate government interest. See Nectow v. City of Cambridge, 277 U.S. 183 (1928); City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr., Inc., 473 U.S. 432 (1985). The Fifth Circuit closed that door twice. It held that homeowners challenging a ban on leasing their homes for fewer than thirty days could not invoke the Due Process Clause at all, because state law did not separately recognize “the right to lease one’s home on a short-term basis.” And it held that, under FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., 508 U.S. 307 (1993), evidence that the ban did nothing to serve the City’s stated interests was “beside the point.” The questions presented are:

  1. Whether a property owner challenging a restriction on a traditional incident of ownership, such as the right to lease, must first identify a state-law entitlement to the precise use restricted before receiving even rational-basis review.

  2. Whether, in rational-basis review of a local land-use restriction, property owners may rebut the government’s asserted justification with record evidence, as in Nectow and Cleburne, or whether Beach Communications forecloses any consideration of that evidence.
