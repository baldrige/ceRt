Questions presented

A parent has a fundamental right under the Free Exercise and Due Process Clauses to direct her child’s religious upbringing. In resolving a religious disagreement between two fit parents, a state court barred a fit mother from taking her child to any church, religious organization or event, including “exposure to the teachings of any religious philosophy or of the Bible in general” without the father’s consent, thus giving the father final authority over any “teachings * * * of the Bible.” Many state courts hold that an order restricting a fit parent’s religious exercise with her child must satisfy strict scrutiny or, at minimum, be supported by an evidentiary showing of substantial harm to the child. The Supreme Judicial Court of Maine diverged, holding that the best-interest-of-the-child standard governs because the order merely allocates decisionmaking authority between two parents who disagree over religion. The questions presented are:

  1. Whether a state court order prohibiting a fit parent from practicing her faith with her child during her custodial time, including attending any church, religious organization or event, or even exposure to the teachings of any religious philosophy or the Bible in general, is subject to strict scrutiny under the Free Exercise and Due Process Clauses, or whether the court may subordinate a parent’s fundamental right to religious exercise to a subjective best-interests determination when the parents disagree about religion.

  2. Whether a custody order violates the Free Exercise Clause pursuant to Masterpiece Cakeshop v. Colorado Civil Rights Commission, 584 U.S. 617 (2018), when it prohibits a fit parent from exercising her Christian faith with her child and demonstrates disagreement at best, or hostility at worst, about Christian doctrine and viewpoints.
