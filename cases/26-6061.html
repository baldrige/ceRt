Zachary Holly v. Arkansas

IFP petition · Supreme Court of Arkansas, No. CR-24-703 · judgment April 16, 2026

Pending

Question presented

In Florida v. Nixon, 543 U.S. 175 (2004), the Court held that the Sixth Amendment does not require counsel to obtain a defendant’s “express consent” to a strategic concession of guilt at a capital trial when that strategy has been “adequately disclosed to and discussed with the defendant” and the defendant does not object to it. Id. at 179. In McCoy v. Louisiana, 584 U.S. 414 (2018), the Court held that counsel usurped his client’s autonomy by conceding guilt over the defendant’s “adamant” objections. Id. at 417. Those objections began with McCoy’s “furious” rejection of a concession strategy that counsel discussed with his client two weeks before trial. Id. at 418–19. In this Arkansas death penalty case, defense counsel conceded the guilt of his cognitively impaired client without first disclosing that strategy to him. The lower courts are divided over whether McCoy or Nixon controls when, as here, a defendant is surprised by counsel’s concession of guilt but does not affirmatively object to counsel or the court. The question presented is: When trial counsel concedes a defendant’s guilt without first consulting the defendant about that strategy, and the defendant does not object at the time of trial, does the lack of an objection prevent the defendant from showing that trial counsel usurped his autonomy under the Sixth Amendment as enforced by McCoy?
