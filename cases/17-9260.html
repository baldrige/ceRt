Teena Colebrook v. CIT Bank

IFP petition · United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, No. 16-56445 · judgment October 6, 2017

Certiorari denied · October 1, 2018

Question presented

This Petition presents related issues of Exceptional Importance to the American Public, due process and the integrity of the Judicial system. It raises the question about whether the District Court erred in finding that Ms. Colebrook was properly served with a motion for an in Rem hearing and issues relating to the 100-year-old Mailbox Rule. Whether the Supreme Court should modify/change the Mailbox Rule to reflect the modern day realties of mounting evidence of mail theft, non-delivery of mail and no guarantee of delivery of first class mail to ensure that notice of court hearings and other important documents are sent by the respective parties via certified or other guaranteed mail services requiring signature of the actual person being served and thereby preserving all parties constitutional right to due process and to be able to face their accusers. This in turn would help to preserve the integrity of the judicial system and avoid the possibility of self-serving, easily fabricated, forged and/or perjured statements of mailing. With today's technology and many other more reliable forms of mail it would eliminate the need for the arguments of weak over strong presumption and ease the burden on the courts of trying to decide the truth of mailing because absolute proof of delivery would be available. To resolve the circuit splits (full discussion of such is on pages 5-12) between the Ninth, Second, Third, Sixth, Seventh and Eighth Circuits where the mailbox rule is a classic "bursting bubble" presumption; whereby the Fifth Circuit (covering Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi, and the Deep South before 1981) holds that the mailbox rule can only be rebutted by clear and convincing evidence. This position is taken in some older Ninth Circuit law but then there is the Seventh Circuit which says, the presumption is rebutted on presentation of evidence of nonreceipt. In holding that the mailbox rule requires rebuttal by clear and convincing evidence, the Ninth Circuit ignored its more recent precedent to follow the Fifth Circuit. The Second Circuit has a similar split within it. The common law mailbox rule articulated by the Tenth Circuit and other courts does not stand for the proposition that a document is filed when mailed but rather that there is a rebuttable presumption of physical delivery. The Third Circuit goes further and considers the operation of the mailbox rule presumption under FRE301 and the circumstances in which the presumption may be rebutted and distinguishes between strong and weak presumptions A "strong presumption" of receipt applies when notice is sent by certified mail, because it creates actual evidence of delivery in the form of a receipt. Santana Gonzalez v. AU 'y Gen., 506 F.3d 274, 279 (3d Cir. 2007) (emphasis added). A "weaker presumption" arises where delivery is sent via regular mail, for which no receipt, or other proof of delivery, is generated. Id.
