Shannon Gilmour v. Juliet Stephens

IFP petition · Supreme Court of Vermont, No. 25-AP-409 · judgment August 21, 2026

Pending

Questions presented

Respondent sued Petitioner and Petitioner’s twelve-year-old child, M.G., for eviction, serving the child with a summons “directed to: Shannon Gilmour on behalf of minor child” that required an answer and any counterclaims within 21 days on pain of default. Petitioner answered and counterclaimed for both. The Vermont trial court held that Petitioner’s appearance as next friend “cures M.G.’s lack of capacity” but that she “may not provide legal representation or act pro se for M.G.,” and that M.G. “currently has no ability to litigate.” It never appointed a guardian ad litem, though its rule provides that a court “shall.” It then dismissed M.G.’s counterclaims because Petitioner had not obtained counsel for M.G. and in the exercise of its “discretionary authority to control its docket,” and dismissed Petitioner’s own related claim because “the claim belongs to M.G.” On appeal, the Vermont Supreme Court held that Petitioner lacked standing to raise any issue concerning M.G.’s claims, and that her argument that she had her own standing — which the trial court had described and rejected — was unpreserved for want of a citation. The questions presented are:

  1. Whether a State violates the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment when its courts permit a child to be made a defendant in a civil action alongside the child’s parent (her sole legal guardian), bar the parent from acting for the child, dismiss the child’s claims for want of counsel the child cannot obtain without appointing the guardian the State’s own rule requires, and then hold that no one has standing to seek review of the dismissal.

  2. Whether a state rule denying a parent standing to appeal the dismissal of a child’s claims is an adequate and independent state ground barring this Court’s review, where the State’s own rules ensured that no one else could raise them.

  3. Whether a state court’s ruling that an argument was unpreserved is an adequate and independent state ground, where the trial court described that argument in its own words and rejected it, and the only thing new on appeal was a citation.
