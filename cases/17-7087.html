Christopher F. Jones v. Tom Schweitzer, Warden

IFP petition · United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, No. 17-3103 · judgment August 30, 2017

Certiorari denied · February 20, 2018

Question presented

The constitutional right to the effective assistance of counsel “is a bedrock principle in our justice system.” Martinez v. Ryan, 132 S. Ct. 1309, 1317 (2012). A petitioner submitting that he has been deprived of that right must show that the attorney’s conduct both was objectively unreasonable and prejudiced him. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984). A person incarcerated as a result of a state conviction may bring federal constitutional claims to the federal courts. 28 U.S.C. § 2254. To demonstrate grounds for relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2254 a petitioner must show, among other things, that the state’s decision denying relief was “contrary to, or involved an unreasonable application of” clearly established Supreme Court precedent. 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d); Williams v. Taylor, 529 U.S. 362, 412-13 (2000). When a state inmate loses his § 2254 petition, he may appeal only if the district or circuit court rules that he has made a substantial showing of the denial of a federal constitutional right. 28 U.S.C. § 2253(c)(1)(A). A petitioner satisfies this standard by demonstrating that reasonable jurists “could disagree with the district court’s resolution of his constitutional claims or that jurists could conclude the issues presented are adequate to deserve encouragement to proceed further.” Buck v. Davis, 137 S. Ct. 759, 773 (2017). The court may not invert the process by deciding the merits of the issue and then refuse to certify the issue for appeal. Id. In this case, Petitioner asserted that his trial counsel was ineffective for failing to call an expert at trial. If the expert had testified consistently with his report, the victim’s credibility would have been challenged on her material claim of sexual abuse. The state appeals court decision denying relief on the grounds that Petitioner had not shown that he was prejudiced was contrary to or unreasonably applied this Court’s case law on showing prejudice, as a reasonable probability existed that the verdict as to that charge would have been different if the expert had been called in Petitioner’s defense. The district court’s denial of the habeas petition was debatable. But when Petitioner sought a certificate of appealability (“COA”), the Sixth Circuit did not review for whether the decision was debatable. Instead, the Sixth Circuit denied COA on the grounds that Petitioner’s claim failed on the merits. The first question presented, then, is whether the Sixth Circuit failed to follow this Court’s clear precedent as to the standard of review for whether a COA should be granted by failing to question whether the district court’s ruling was debatable and worthy of further review and, instead, skipping that question and focusing solely on the merits of the underlying claim. The second question is whether a court of appeals errs under this Court’s standards for issuing a COA when a district court’s ruling that defense counsel’s failure to subpoena a witness did not prejudice the defendant was, at the very least, debatable, in light of this Court’s precedent as to what is required to show that a defendant is prejudiced by an attorney’s failure to subpoena a witness.
