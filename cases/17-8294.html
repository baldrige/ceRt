Steven Allen Weldon v. Michael Pacheco, Warden, et al.

IFP petition · United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, No. 17-8030 · judgment November 6, 2017

Certiorari denied · May 14, 2018

Question presented

: QUESTION ONE: If a United States District Court erroneously applies a procedural bar on a Habeas Corpus Petition filed pursuant to 28 USCS §2254 which precludes federal habeas review, does such an error deprive the Petitioner of Constitutional Due Process and is such an error therefore proper under Federal Rules of Civil Procedure Rule 60(b)(4) as stated in Gonzalez v. Crosby, 545 US 524, 162 L Ed 2d 480,125 S Ct 2641? QUESTION TWO: Is a substantive due process mental competency claim that a defendant was in fact convicted and sentenced while mentally incompetent (Drope v. Missouri,, 420 US 162, 43 L Ed 2d 103, 95 S Ct 896) subject to a procedural default precluding federal habeas corpus review 28 U.S. C. §2254? QUESTION THREE: Do the due process and equal protection clauses of the United States constitution allow a circuit court to change substantive law for a single case and thereafter revert back? In other words: may the Tenth Circuit state that a substantive due process mental competency claim may not be subject to a procedural bar to federal habeas corpus relief and then proceed to enforce a procedural bar on that exact same issue for a single case only to turn around and reinstate the rule that such a claim cannot be procedurally barred twenty-eight (28) days later? (Compare: Sena v. New Mexico, 109 F.3d 652, 654 (10th Cir. 1997); Weldon v. Wyo. Dept Corr., 1997 U.S. App. LEXIS 28340; and Nguyen v. Reynolds, 131 F.3d 1340) QUESTION FOUR: If a district court erroneously applies a procedural bar to a substantive due process mental competency claim and bars federal habeas corpus relief under 28 U.S.0 §2254; can it be said under the Fourteenth Amendment that the habeas petitioner was given the due process right to be heard as contemplated under the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States constitution? Does the simple act of filing a habeas corpus petition pursuant to 28 U.S.C. §2254 fulfill the substantive aspects of constitutional due process? QUESTION FIVE: Do the due process and equal protection clauses of the united states constitution allow a federal district court to apply an “anticipatory procedural bar” (See Moore v. Schoernan, 288 F.3d 1231, 1233 n.3 (10th Cir. 2002) (‘”Anticipatory procedural bar’ occurs when the federal courts apply procedural bar to an unexhausted claim that would be procedurally barred under state law if the petitioner returned to state court to exhaust it.”) which effectively usurps a state court’s jurisdiction and goes against the holding of this honorable court in Stewart v. MartinezVillareal, 523 US 637, 118 5 Ct 1618; wherein the Court there stated: “This Court’s cases have never suggested that a prisoner whose habeas petition was dismissed for failure to exhaust state remedies, and who then did exhaust those remedies and returned to federal court, was by such action filing a successive petition.” Ibid, at 140 L Ed 2d 853. QUESTION SIX: If a district court procedurally bars a state habeas petitioner for failing to “adequately present” his claims to the state courts and the petitioner returns to the state courts pursuant to Stewart v. Martinez- Villareal, 523 US 637, 118 S Ct 1618; and receives a merits determination from both the state district court and the state supreme court; is the habeas petitioner deprived of constitutional due process if the district and circuit courts continue to enforce the procedural bar? QUESTION SEVEN: In a long series of recent cases, this honorable court has granted certiorari based upon violations of substantive rules of constitutional and statutory law stating: “Substantive rules set forth categorical constitutional guarantees that place certain criminal laws and punishments altogether beyond a state’s power to impose. It follows that when a state enforces a proscription or penalty barred by the Constitution, the resulting conviction or sentence is, by definition, unlawful. (Kennedy, J., joined by Roberts, Ch. J., and Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan, JJ.)” Ibid, Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 US , 136 S Ct , 193 L Ed 2d 599 at Hn.1O. “A conviction or sentence imposed in violation of a substantive rule is not just erroneous but contrary to law and, as a result, void. It follows, as a general principle, that a court has no authority to leave in place a conviction or sentence that violates a substantive rule, regardless of whether the conviction or sentence became final before the rule was announced.” Ibid, Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 US, 136SCt , 193 L Ed 2d 599 at Hn. 12. “An unconstitutional law is void, and is as no law. A penalty imposed pursuant to an unconstitutional law is no less void because the prisoner’s sentence became final before the law was held unconstitutional. There is no grandfather clause that permits states to enforce punishments the Constitution forbids. To conclude otherwise would undercut the Constitution’s substantive guarantees. No circumstances call more for the invocation of a rule of complete retroactivity than when the conduct being penalized is constitutionally immune from punishment. The same principle should govern the application of substantive rules on collateral review. Where a state lacks the power to proscribe a habeas petitioner’s conduct, it cannot constitutionally insist that he remain in jail.” Ibid, Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 US , 136 S Ct , 193 L Ed 2d 599 at Hn.13. “The retroactive application of substantive rules does not implicate a state’s weighty interests in ensuring the finality of convictions and sentences. Teague warns against the intrusiveness of continually forcing the states to marshal resources in order to keep in prison defendants whose trials and appeals conformed to then-existing constitutional standards. This concern has no application in the realm of substantive rules, for no resources marshaled by a state could preserve a conviction or sentence that the Constitution deprives the state of power to impose.” Ibid, Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 US , 136 S Ct 193 LEd 2d 599 at Hn.15.
