Mary Beth Harcrow v. Clyde Harcrow, et al.

IFP petition · United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, No. 21-5943 · judgment September 19, 2022

Certiorari denied · October 2, 2023

Questions presented

:

  1. Pursuant to Supreme Court Rule 10(a), the Question Presented herein is whether the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals erred in adopting the ruling of the Middle District Court of Tennessee which entered incorrect rulings on an important federal question, namely a Federal statute, by incorrectly failing to recognize and enforce in Tennessee a domestic violence order issued in Delaware in favor of Petitioner Mary Beth Harcrow (“Petitioner”) pursuant to the Violence Against Women Act (18 U.S.C. § 2265) (“VAWA”), in which both courts failed and refused to acknowledge, recognize, order judicial notice thereof, enforce, or otherwise give the required full faith and credit to a valid Delaware domestic violence protective order issued in favor of the Petitioner pursuant to VAWA (the “March 2018 Protective Order (PFA)” (Appendix J) entered in Sumner County in March, 2018, following a contested hearing in Delaware on March 9, 2018.

  2. The Sixth Circuit ruling in this case denying Petitioner’s appeal: (A) ignores and disregards plain language mandating recognition and enforcement of PFAs in Federal statutes (18 U.S.C. § 2265); (B) ignores and disregards plain language in Tennessee statutes mandating recognition and enforcement of PFAs (TCA § 36-3-611, TCA § 36-3-618, and TCA § 36-3-622); Petition for Certiorari to the Sixth Circuit Court ofAppeals Harcrow v. Harcrow et al. Case No. 21-5943 ) ( C) ignores and disregards the plain language of the decision of the Supreme Court of Tennessee mandating enforcement of PFA’s as set forth in the certified decision of the Supreme Court of Tennessee directly on point (Matthews v. Pickett, 996 S. W. 2d 162, 164 (Tenn.

  3. (Appendix K)); and (D) ignores and disregards the public policy and plain legislative intent as set forth in TCA § 36-3-618.

  4. Instead, throughout this case, both the Sixth Circuit and the Middle District of Tennessee courts have avoided and evaded the simple truth that the Petitioner was supposedly protected in Tennessee by a valid domestic violence protective order pursuant to the Violence Against Women Act (18 U.S.C. § 2265) (“VAWA”) which both courts treated as non-existent and meaningless in spite of applicable law.

  5. As set forth above, the Supreme Court of Tennessee, by certified opinion, and the Tennessee legislature have both affirmed that, not only are domestic violence protective orders issued by sister states pursuant to VAWA fully enforceable in Tennessee, which must be given full faith and credit in Tennessee, but also that the existence of a domestic violence protective order issued pursuant to VAWA gave rise, in accordance with expressed Tennessee public policy and Petition for Certiorari to the Sixth Circuit Court ofAppeals Harcrow v. Harcrow et al. Case No. 21-5943 legislative intent, to a “special duty to protect,” which special duty extended to the protection of property. Matthews v. Pickett, 996 S. W. 2d 162, 164 (Tenn. 1999)(Appendix K).

  6. Tennessee further extends the “special duty to protect” holders of domestic violence protective orders by mandating the arrest of violators of such orders. TCA § 36-3-618 (Appendix L); TCA § 36-3-622 (Appendix M); and TCA § 36- 3-611 (Appendix N).

  7. The State of Tennessee and its Supreme Court have thus made the clear public policy decision that holders of domestic violence protective orders issued pursuant to 18 U.S.C. § 2265 are not only entitled to have such protective orders given full faith and credit and be fully enforceable in Tennessee, but also that holders of such orders are entitled to a “special duty to protect.” as above, which special duty extends to the protection of property. Matthews v. Pickett, 996 S. W. 2d 162, 164 (Tenn. 1999)(Appendix K).

  8. In this appeal, both the Sixth Circuit and the Middle District Court of Tennessee have repeatedly ignored Petitioner’s multiple attempts to have a certified copy of the “March 2018 Protective Order (PFA)” recognized by taking judicial notice thereof (Appendices F, G, H, and I), which result evades the plain meaning and intent behind the language of the certified opinion of the Tennessee Petition for Certiorari to the Sixth Circuit Court ofAppeals Harcrow v. Harcrow et al. Case No. 21-5943 Supreme Court set forth in Matthews above, and the Tennessee public policy enunciated in TCA § 36-3-618 providing for enhanced protection for holders of domestic violence protective orders including the Petitioner.

  9. By evading and ignoring the plain meaning of the relevant statutes, and by failing and refusing to recognize or enforce Petitioner’s “March 2018 Protective Order (PFA)”(Appendix J). the Sixth Circuit has in this case incorrectly gone against the stated intent of Congress and the Tennessee legislature and reduced Petitioner’s domestic violence protective order to a “meaningless piece of paper,’’which failure to enforce the U.S. Department of Justice has characterized as being even more dangerous for domestic violence victims by “creating a false sense of security.” U.S. Department of Justice Legal Series Bulletin #4, Enforcement of Protective Orders 1, p. 5 (2002).

  10. Where Congress, the Tennessee legislature, and the Tennessee Supreme Court have plainly required full enforcement, a domestic violence order issued pursuant to 18 U.S.C. § 2265 creates an entitlement that cannot be taken away without due process, per the Fourteenth Amendment.

  11. For the foregoing reasons, the decisions of the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals in this case which failed and/or refused to recognize and enforce the Petitioner’s “March 2018 Protective Order (PFA) issued pursuant to Petition for Certiorari to the Sixth Circuit Court ofAppeals Harcrow v.
