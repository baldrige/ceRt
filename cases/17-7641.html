Obel Cruz-Garcia v. Texas

IFP petition · Court of Criminal Appeals of Texas, No. WR-85,051-02, WR-85,051-03 · judgment November 1, 2017

Certiorari denied · June 4, 2018

Question presented

During the penalty phase of Mr. Cruz-Garcia’s capital murder trial, the trial court engaged in an in-chambers conversation with a juror who was later revealed to be the lone holdout for a life sentence and gave that juror an impermissible instruction without counsel present. In his initial state habeas application challenging his capital murder conviction and death sentence, Mr. Cruz-Garcia raised, among other claims, a judicial misconduct claim asserting that the judge’s conduct violated his due process rights to a fair trial. Because the habeas judge was the same judge who presided over the trial and whose conduct was at issue, Mr. CruzGarcia filed a recusal motion but that motion was denied without a hearing. The conflicted habeas judge then ruled on the merits of all of the claims in Mr. Cruz-Garcia’s habeas application without providing him any opportunity to prove his case by introducing evidence to support his claims. The State, by contrast, was allowed to submit controverting evidence that was never subjected to adversarial testing. Further, the habeas judge disposed of several of Mr. Cruz-Garcia’s claims, including the judicial misconduct claim, by relying on her personal recollection, without giving Mr. Cruz-Garcia notice of what the recollections were and the opportunity to determine if there was evidence that contradicted them. These procedures violated the mandatory statutorily enumerated procedure that governs Texas capital habeas proceedings. The judge entered her finding of facts and conclusions of law, which recommended denying Mr. Cruz-Garcia relief. The Texas Court of Criminal Appeals rubberstamped the process, presumptively overruling all of Mr. Cruz-Garcia’s objections to the state habeas court process and violations of due process, summarily affirming the state habeas court’s facially inadequate findings and conclusions. Thus, this case presents the following question: In a death penalty case, where a state provides a post-conviction procedure for challenging the constitutional violations that occurred at a capital trial, and where the state court violated the mandatory state procedures, do the minimal due process protections of having a neutral fact-finder, the opportunity to be provided with notice, and the opportunity to present evidence and challenge adverse evidence apply?
