Question presented

“[T]he First Amendment has its fullest and most urgent application to speech uttered during a campaign for political office.” Burson v. Freeman, 504 U.S. 191, 196 (1992). Despite that core protection, Washington has imposed extraordinary disclosure obligations (backed by stiff fines) on digital platforms that make it financially irrational for them to disseminate political advertisements. Platforms must provide any member of the public who demands it a litany of details about the political advertisements they display within two business days. The chilling effect is undeniable: The requirements are so burdensome that Meta, Google, and others have stopped disseminating political advertisements in Washington altogether. While the burden this regime imposes on political speech is overwhelming, the public demand for the mandated information is virtually nonexistent. Between 2018 and 2021, Meta received requests under the law from just three people. Meta produced most (but not all) of the information, but the state sued anyway and sought and obtained an astonishing $35 million fine—the largest campaign-finance penalty in U.S. history. In a fractured opinion, the Washington Supreme Court found no First Amendment violation, expressly disagreeing with a Fourth Circuit decision holding a similar Maryland regime unconstitutional. The question presented is: Whether the First Amendment permits a state to impose onerous content- and speaker-based disclosure requirements on third-party platforms that make it financially irrational for those platforms to disseminate political speech.
