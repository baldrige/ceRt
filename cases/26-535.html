Question presented

In Taylor v. Sturgell, 553 U.S. 880 (2008), this Court explained that the rule against nonparty preclusion is subject to exceptions that “can be grouped into six categories,” while declining to offer a “definitive taxonomy.” The courts of appeals are divided over whether those six categories are the exclusive grounds for nonparty claim preclusion, or whether a nonparty defendant may also invoke claim preclusion based on a “close and significant relationship” — such as a parentsubsidiary relationship — with the defendant in a prior action. In the decision below, the Sixth Circuit held the six categories exclusive and rejected the First and Eighth Circuits’ contrary approach as “unpersuasive.” The questions presented are: I. Whether the six categories of exceptions to the rule against nonparty preclusion recognized in Taylor v. Sturgell are the exclusive grounds on which a nonparty defendant may assert claim preclusion, or whether a defendant that shares a close and significant relationship — such as a parent-subsidiary relationship — with the defendant in a prior action may also invoke claim preclusion to bar a successive suit on the same claim. II. Whether, even if Taylor’s six categories are exclusive, the Sixth Circuit erred by holding that the control exception is unavailable unless the nonpar ty controlled the prior litigation to the exclusion of the named party — a requirement that conflicts with Taylor and Montana v. United States, 440 U.S. 147 (1979), which ask only whether the nonparty had a “laboring oar” in that litigation.
