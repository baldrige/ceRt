Matthew Stephen Akins v. Daniel K. Knight, et al.

IFP petition · United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, No. 16-3555 · judgment July 25, 2017

Certiorari denied · February 20, 2018

Question presented

I. Whether to resolve the split between the 8th Circuit and the First, Third, Fifth, Seventh, Ninth and Eleventh Circuits of the U.S. Court of Appeals, created by the 8th Circuit’s affirmation of the Dist. Court’s ruling the First Amendment does not grant Americans’ a right to film a peaceably assembled citizen petitioning the government for a redress of grievances and/or film the police in a public lobby. In Akins, the Dist. Court held, “Akins also argues that he was retaliated against when he was stopped from filming a citizen in the Police Department lobby in 2011 . . . Further, he has no constitutional right to videotape any public proceedings he wishes to. See Rice v. Kempker, 374 F.3d 675, 678 (8th Cir. 2004) “[N]either the public nor the media has a First Amendment right to videotape, photograph, or make audio recordings of government proceedings that are by law open to the public.”(opinion p 34) A ruling contrary to 6 other federal circuit courts of appeal which have held that the First Amendment does grant citizens’ the right to film the police in public especially those filing a misconduct complaint against police officers to comply with the First and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution. II. Whether to resolve a question of exceptional national importance created by the 8th Circuit’s affirmation of the District Court’s ruling the First Amendment does not grant Americans the right to uncensored access to the “Designated Public Forum” of the Columbia Police Facebook page where Akins had posted six CFJ reports during a debate over the oversight authority of the Citizens Police Review Board (CPRB) and those posts were censored and subsequently all non-police approved posts were thereafter prohibited without notice or a hearing from this public forum. The District Court held: “his links to the Citizens for Justice page were removed from the Police Department’s Facebook page in the summer of 2011; . . . links to the Police Department’s Facebook page were treated the same as everyone else’s and there is no constitutional right to unlimited posting.” (p 34-5) establishing an internet censorship regime without requiring the government to provide a compelling justification for censorship or for changing an open designated public forum to a censored one if these acts are in violation of the First and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution. III. Whether to resolve a question of exceptional national importance as to the application of judicial disqualification and a federal statute requiring a verified motion citing “good cause” to be ruled on by an unrelated jurist due to 8th Circuit’s ruling that a Judicial disqualification under statute 28 U.S.C. §144 could be adjudicated under the standards laid out for a 28 U.S.C § 455 merits disqualification determination. 28 U.S.C. §144 required Judge Laughrey to assign the motion to disqualify for bias under 28 U.S.C. §144 to another judge for ruling. The Eighth Circuit’s ruling merged the 28 U.S.C. §144 and 28 U.S.C. §455 standards at the challenged judge’s discretion. In Akins’: 1. Case evidence related directly critical reporting about Judge Laughrey’s ruling on prior Columbia Police case; 2. Judge Laughrey’s husband held an office of trust with the City of Columbia during the case; 3. Akins’ attorney Stephen Wyse had previously filed a judicial misconduct complaint against Judge Laughrey and had on-going conflict with the judge and her husband. Was the court’s ruling on the verified motion to disqualify Judge Laughrey by Judge Laughrey a violation of 28 U.S.C. § 144. Further were sufficient grounds raised under 28 U.S.C. § 455 to disqualify Judge Laughrey based upon at least an appearance of impropriety if these acts are in violation of the above federal statutes and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution.
