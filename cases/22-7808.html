Elroy Pedro Gomez v. William Joe Sullivan, Warden

IFP petition · United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, No. 21-16999 · judgment January 26, 2023

Certiorari denied · October 2, 2023

Questions presented

Petitioner raises the following questions:

  1. In the first place, was Petitioner denied his Sixth Amendment right to the effective assistance of counsel when his court-appointed trial attorney failed to advise him that he had no defense to the ADW charge or the sentencing enhancements and was certain to be found guilty of those charges and to be sentenced to a longer term than the prosecution was offering, and, therefore, had no rational choice but to accept the offer, but, instead, advised him that they were going to beat the case?

  2. Was Petitioner denied his Fifth and Fourteenth Amendment rights to due process and equal protection when, on habeas corpus in the California Court of Appeal and Supreme Court, those courts failed to follow California Supreme Court decisional law (see, e.g., People v. Duvall, 9 Cal.4th 464, 474-475 (1995); People v. Pope, 23 Cal.3d 412, 426 (1979)), which requires the courts, when a petitioner alleges facts which make out a prima facie case of an unlawful conviction, to issue an order to show case, and if those factual allegations are contested, to conduct an evidentiary hearing in order to resolve them?

  3. Were the summary denials of Petitioner’s state-court habeas petitions – without issuance of an order to show cause, an evidentiary hearing, and a statement of reasons – per se unreasonable and, therefore, not entitled to deference on habeas corpus in the federal courts under 28 U.S.C. § 2254 (d)(2) because those state courts resolved factual issues and Petitioner’s credibility without affording him an evidentiary hearing, as held in Nunes v. Mueller, 350 F.3d 1045, 1054 (9th Cir. 2003) and other decisions of the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals?

  4. Was Petitioner denied his Fifth Amendment right to due process when, on habeas corpus in the United States District Court, the court issued an order to show cause, and Petitioner made the showing stated in Paragraph E below (p. v) – specifically that his attorney failed to give him competent advice to accept the prosecution’s offer of a plea bargain and that, with such advice, he would have accepted it – but the court not only denied an evidentiary hearing to determine the truth of the factual showing and the credibility of Petitioner, as required by Ninth Circuit law (e.g. Nunes v. Mueller, supra, 350 F.3d 1045, 1054), but resolved those issues and denied the petition without a hearing?

  5. Was Petitioner denied his Fifth Amendment and Fourteenth Amendment rights to appellate due process and equal protection when a three-judge panel of the Ninth Circuit, in a 3 1/2-page “Memorandum” decision, affirmed the judgment of the District Court, without addressing Petitioner’s contention that Ninth Circuit law requires an evidentiary hearing to resolve factual issues where the petitioner alleges facts and makes showings which are not unreasonable or refuted by the record and, if true, would entitle him to relief or even citing the controlling case, Nunes v. Mueller, 350 F.3d 1045? And:

  6. Should this Court summarily reverse the decision of the Ninth Circuit and remand the matter to that court with directions to order the District Court to conduct an evidentiary hearing on Petitioner’s factual claims? Where: A. Petitioner was charged with attempted premeditated murder (carrying a life sentence with a minimum of 7 years, doubled under the “second strike” provision of the Three Strikes Law to 14 to life), assault with a deadly weapon (2, 3, or 4 years, doubled to 4, 6, or 8 years), sentence enhancement for use of a deadly weapon (1 year), enhancement for infliction of great bodily injury (3 years), prior “strike” conviction (requiring doubling of terms), enhancement for such prior conviction (5 years), and possession of ammunition (16 months if consecutive), B. The prosecution offered Petitioner a plea bargain for dismissal of the attempted murder charge and a lesser term of 9 years, in exchange for his plea of guilty to ADW and admission of the GBI enhancement and of the prior “strike” (i.e., not the 5-year enhancement for the prior conviction but of the fact that it was of a serious felony, invoking the “second-strike” provision of the Three Strikes Law). C. Petitioner had absolutely no defense to any of the charges in bold type1 and no possibility of any sentence less than 13 years (4 years for ADW, 1 year for use of a deadly weapon, 3 years for infliction of GBI, and 5 years for the prior) or 14 years, 4 months, if a consecutive term were mandatory for the illegal possession of ammunition.2 D. Petitioner had a trial by jury which found him guilty of all of the charges, and the court sentenced him to 14 years to life for the attempted 1 His only defense to the attempted murder charge was that, in spite of his statement to the victim that he was going to kill him, he could have killed if he actually intended to (see ER-2 40 ll. 22-28), which made it probable that, if the jury found that he did so intend (i.e., was guilty of attempted murder), it would also find that the attempt was “first degree” – i.e., deliberate, premeditated murder -- which under Penal Code section 654 increased the penalty from 5, 7, or 9 years to “life” (minimum of 7 years), doubled under the second-strike provisions of the Three Strikes Law. 2 The Three Strikes Law forbids any sentence other than a prison sentence (e.g., probation) for a defendant found to have been convicted of a prior “strike” (Cal. Pen. Code, § 1170.12, subd. (a)(2)) and requires consecutive prison terms for crimes “not arising from the same set of operative facts.” (Subd. (a)(6).)
