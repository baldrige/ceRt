Question presented

SafeSport is a private corporation with the power to make federal law: its policies “apply as though they were incorporated in and made a part of” the United States Code. 36 U.S.C. § 220541(b). SafeSport sanctioned Petitioners for violating its policies. Petitioners then sued, arguing that Congress delegated governmental power to a private entity. Alternatively, if SafeSport is a governmental actor, Petitioners argued that SafeSport violated due process by not giving notice and a hearing before sanctioning them. The district court brushed aside § 220541(b)—the provision that gives SafeSport legislative power—calling it “not a model of clarity.” The court of appeals did not even address the provision, holding instead that Petitioners “lack standing to raise a non-delegation claim” because their due process claim failed. In other words, the court held that Petitioners could not bring a private nondelegation claim without also having a separate meritorious constitutional claim. Even SafeSport was confused by that reasoning and chose not to adopt it in related litigation. The questions presented are: Whether, to have standing to bring a private nondelegation claim, a person must also have a separate meritorious constitutional claim? Whether Congress unconstitutionally delegated legislative and other government powers to SafeSport in the Ted Stevens Olympic and Amateur Sports Act (ASA), 36 U.S.C. chapter 2205? Whether SafeSport is a governmental actor subject to the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause?
