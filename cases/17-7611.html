William T. Montgomery v. Ohio

IFP petition · Court of Appeals of Ohio, Lucas County, No. L-15-1282 · judgment October 28, 2016

Certiorari denied · April 2, 2018

Question presented

(CAPITAL CASE) The State of Ohio is poised to execute an innocent man. Petitioner William T. Montgomery, (“W.T. Montgomery”) is going to be executed on April 11, 2018 for the aggravated murders of Debra Ogle (“Ogle”) and Cynthia Tincher (“Tincher”). At trial, the prosecution argued a very fact specific timeline of events wherein W.T. Montgomery murdered both girls on March 8, 1986. The prosecution’s timeline specifically argued that Ogle was murdered first, and then Tincher was murdered second in order to cover up the tracks of Ogle murder. This timeline was the centerpiece of the prosecution’s case and was derived from Glover Heard (“Heard”), a co-defendant who made a deal with the prosecution. In 2007, the federal district court granted W.T. Montgomery’s habeas petition and ordered a new trial because the prosecution withheld exculpatory evidence (a police report in which a classmate of Debra Ogle reported seeing her alive prior to 1:20 a.m. on March 12) that could challenge the accuracy of the prosecution’s timeline and sequence of the murders. On appeal, the original panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit affirmed, but upon en banc review, a divided court (10-5) held that the withheld police report was not substantially material to warrant reversal. But the district court opinion that was reviewed by the Sixth Circuit was incomplete. After the Sixth Circuit reversed the district court’s grant of habeas relief, W.T. Montgomery, for the first time, obtained crucial scientific evidence – an expert forensic report – that confirmed that the prosecution’s trial timeline was false. i 3587146.1 Because it would have also shattered the prosecution’s timeline, this new piece of evidence would have confirmed the materiality of the withheld police report. In describing the impact of this expert report, the state prosecutors recently stated: Establishing at trial that Ogle was not killed on March 8th would have delivered a severe blow to the State’s case because it would have directly contradicted Heard’s testimony, it would have contradicted the prosecution’s time line of the murders and it would have eliminated defendant’s motivation for killing Tincher to cover up his murder of Ogle. (Appellee brief at p. 15). Armed with this expert report, W.T. Montgomery attempted to come back and have an Ohio court look at this exculpatory evidence for the first time, but even though the state appellate court determined that W.T. Montgomery was “unavoidably prevented” from developing the expert report sooner, the Ohio courts refused to even consider this new evidence that confirmed W.T. Montgomery was convicted on a false set of facts. Instead of reviewing the merits of this new expert report and the fact that it confirmed the materiality of the withheld police report, the Ohio courts relied on a new arbitrarily created barrier to Ohio criminal defendants seeking a new trial. Specifically, the Ohio court of appeals held that, even though this case had been pending for 30 years, W.T. Montgomery’s attorney waited too long (nine months) to file the motion for new trial in state court. In other words, even in the absence of any prejudice to the prosecution, a defendant—who was cheated by the prosecution at trial, was “unavoidably prevented” from developing evidence that confirms the materiality of withheld evidence, and was sentenced to death on a false ii 3587146.1 set of facts—now faces a judicially-created, uncodified and arbitrary statute of limitations dictating when a motion for new trial to rectify an unfair trial must be filed. Based on this narrative, the question presented for review is: Does Ohio’s arbitrary, undefined standard for filing delayed motions for new trial violate the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses of the United States Constitution? iii 3587146.1
