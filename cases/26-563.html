Jay Folse v. John McCuskey, Jr., et al.

Questions presented

Petitioner criticized West Virginia’s State Auditor and a deputy over the office’s refusal to deliver tax deeds he had purchased. A state magistrate’s personal safety order barred him from “verbal contact” with the Auditor’s office and directed that “[a]ll communication shall be via email or USPS.” A Capitol Police officer then swore out a criminal complaint charging petitioner with violating that order. The sworn basis was four acts: petitioner sued the Auditor and the deputy, noticed their depositions, criticized the Auditor on the Auditor’s public Facebook page, and emailed the deputy. A magistrate issued an arrest warrant. Petitioner was arrested at a public tax auction and jailed, and the prosecutor then dismissed the charge. When petitioner sued under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, both courts below held that the warrant established probable cause and that probable cause defeated every claim against every defendant. The questions presented are:

  1. Whether a magistrate’s probable-cause finding conclusively insulates the officials who procured an arrest warrant from First Amendment retaliation and Fourth Amendment malicious-prosecution claims—a question that has divided the courts of appeals—even when the warrant application describes only protected petitioning and speech.

  2. Whether a court may hold at summary judgment, as a matter of law, that a citizen criminally violated a court order by sending emails the order’s own terms expressly permitted.

  3. Whether qualified immunity may be granted without identifying the constitutional right at issue or deciding whether that right was clearly established.
