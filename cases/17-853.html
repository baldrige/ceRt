Natalia Leshchenko Wilson v. Eric Aldridge, Warden

Paid petition · United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, No. 16-7628 · judgment September 13, 2017

Certiorari denied · February 20, 2018
At-filing estimate: 1% cert probability

Before the decision, well below the 4.1% base rate, with no standout signals pointing toward a grant.

Questions presented

  1. RELEVANT ISSUES: The appeals court denied further proceedings in this case when it refused the issuance of a certificate of appealability (“COA”). A COA may issue when an applicant has made “a substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional right.” 28 U.S.C. § 2253(c)(2). In light of the evidence that petitioner’s will was overborne by the endeavors of her trial counsel to induce her guilty plea to murder, reasonable jurists could well debate whether Natalia Leshchenko Wilson was afforded due protections under the Sixth Amendment. QUESTION 1: Whether the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit improperly denied a certificate of appealability, deciding an important federal question in a manner that conflicts with relevant decisions of this Court?

  2. RELEVANT ISSUES: Petitioner pleaded guilty to a double murder and received two life sentences, despite the protestation of innocence she expressed to trial counsel. The longstanding test for determining the validity of a guilty plea is “whether the plea represents a voluntary and intelligent choice among the alternative courses of action open to the defendant.” North Carolina v. Alford, 400 U.S. 25, 31 (1970). Pleading guilty was not a voluntary and intelligent choice among the alternative courses of action open to petitioner where her counsel’s conduct was aimed at forcing petitioner to admit guilt, all the while petitioner had little to gain by avoiding trial. -iQUESTION 2: Whenever a person charged with murder faces the prospect of capital punishment, is it unreasonable for defendant to reject a plea offer that would result in a life sentence, even if the accused protests her innocence?

  3. RELEVANT ISSUES: The performance of petitioner’s counsel fell below an objective standard of reasonableness during the plea process because trial attorneys induced petitioner’s guilty plea by exerting pressure on petitioner’s daughter and her priest. The performance of counsel falls below the Strickland objective standard of reasonableness if counsel’s specific acts or omissions are not demonstrably the result of actual strategic choices made between or among all plausible options after thorough investigation of the law and the facts. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 691 (1984). Petitioner was prejudiced by the objectively unreasonable performance of counsel during pretrial proceedings when counsel compelled her to plead guilty because pressuring third parties to prevail on a defendant to plead guilty is not a legitimate trial strategy. QUESTION 3: Whether petitioner’s conviction is violative of her Sixth Amendment right to effective assistance of counsel?

  4. RELEVANT ISSUES: The forensic evidence that the DNA found on petitioner’s pants did not necessarily belong to the victims was never explored at trial or any pretrial proceeding, and the district court similarly declined an evidentiary hearing. A habeas petitioner is entitled to a federal evidentiary hearing if she - ii - can show cause for her failure to develop the facts at an earlier proceeding and actual prejudice resulting from that failure. Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes, 504 U.S. 1, 11 (1992). The central piece of evidence that the Commonwealth relied upon in making a reference to the victim’s blood on petitioner’s clothing was a report of a laboratory analysis identifying DNA in an alleged spot of blood on the pants that Wilson wore to the police station on the night of her interrogation to be that of the victim. The five-year delay in analyzing the statistical probabilities of the DNA evidence was due to a backlog of cases within the Virginia Department of Forensic Science. QUESTION 4: Whether an evidentiary hearing is necessary and would be useful to this Court?
