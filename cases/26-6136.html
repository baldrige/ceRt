Daniel Mackey v. Joanna Olejniczak

IFP petition · Court of Appeal of California, Fourth Appellate District, Division Three, No. G066480 · judgment April 16, 2026

Pending

Questions presented

Former Judge Israel Claustro presided over Petitioner’s domestic-violence and child-custody hearing in July 2024 while concealing a federal felony to which he pleaded guilty in January 2026, resigning from the bench the same day. By then the time to appeal his orders had long expired. The California Court of Appeal dismissed Petitioner’s appeals as untimely, held that “[a]ppellant may not create appellate jurisdiction for untimely appeals by asserting the challenged orders are void,” expressly declined to say whether the orders were void, and noted that a motion in the trial court remained available without expressing any view on its merit. The Supreme Court of California denied review. The question presented is: Whether the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a State to afford some procedural mechanism, subject to appellate review, through which a litigant may raise a substantial claim that the judge who decided his case was constitutionally disqualified by criminal conduct the judge concealed, where the facts supporting the claim could not have been discovered until after the ordinary time to appeal had run, and where the State’s appellate courts have dismissed the claim as untimely without reaching it, pointing only to a trial-court motion whose availability for such a claim is unsettled under state law. Subsidiary questions, should the Court find that the above warrants plenary review:

  1. Whether Rippo v. Baker, 580 U.S. 285 (2017)—which holds that the Due Process Clause guarantees an objectively impartial tribunal—together with the principle this Court applied in Bracy v. Gramley, 520 U.S. 899 (1997), in permitting a habeas petitioner to develop a comparable bias claim through discovery before being held to his ultimate burden of proof, is satisfied by a State’s direction to file a fresh motion in the trial court under a provision whose availability for a claim of this kind is unsettled under state law, with appellate review available only from a denial and no tolling of the clock that has run in the interim.

  2. Whether Bowles v. Russell, 551 U.S. 205 (2007), which holds that courts lack authority to create equitable exceptions to a jurisdictional filing deadline, applies with the same force where the litigant’s claim could not have been formulated at all before the deadline expired, because the fact giving rise to it was concealed by the very judicial officer whose conduct is at issue—or whether that circumstance instead bears on when such a claim accrues, a question Bowles did not present or decide.
